When Grants Feel Like Security but Aren't

25 May 2026 · 8 min read · Dr Jess Hodgson · Originally published on LinkedIn

Two conversations have been sitting side by side in my head this week.

The first was with a small nonprofit I work with. The funding mix is heavily weighted towards grants, close to all of it. There is no email platform set up, no donor list to speak of, a website that has not been updated in some time, and not much happening on social either.

The second was a few months back. A different small nonprofit told me they raise around $2 million a year. No grants. None. Just email, print, and social. I had to sit with that one for a second.

The comparison would not be fair to either of them. They do different work, they serve different communities. That gap is what I keep coming back to, because it tells me something about how a small nonprofit decides what counts as a foundational investment and what gets treated as optional.

Grants aren't security. They're concentrated exposure.

Grant funding feels solid. The timelines are clear. The amounts are known up front. You can plan around it, build a calendar around it, hire against it. That sense of solidity is one of the reasons small organisations lean so heavily into grants in the first place.

But every dollar of grant income arrives with conditions. Reporting obligations. Criteria that can shift between rounds. A funder relationship that can change next year for reasons you don't control. And when a single income stream represents 99% of your funding, the strength of that stream is also the weakness. Concentration in any one source is exposure, regardless of how that source feels day to day.

Insight

Diversification is risk management, not aspiration

Diversification isn't a nice aspiration for nonprofits that have the bandwidth. It's the basic shape of a financially resilient organisation.

And this is where I keep getting stuck. When a small nonprofit routes almost all of its energy into grants and almost none into the systems that would build a donor base, that's not a resource problem. It's a strategic decision, even when it doesn't feel like one.

What the sector data actually shows

Three reports have been sitting open on my screen for the past fortnight. The ACNC Australian Charities Report, 11th edition. What Not-for-Profit Leaders Need to Know in 2026 from the Institute of Community Directors Australia. The State of Nonprofit Marketing 2025 from Feathr. Read together, they tell a story that the headline figures don't quite show on their own.

The Australian charity sector reached a record $222 billion in revenue in 2023, up 10.7%, according to the ACNC. Donations and bequests jumped by $5 billion to $18.9 billion. On paper, that looks like growth.

The detail underneath is the part that matters. The ACNC reports a single $4.9 billion donation to the Minderoo Foundation group, the largest single donation ever recorded by the ACNC.

Strip that one gift out, and donations across the entire Australian charity sector rose by less than 0.4%. That is $54 million across the whole sector in a year.

ACNC Australian Charities Report, 11th edition

What that means, practically, is that the sector didn't grow. One relationship grew. One gift. Everything else held flat, or fell.

The ICDA data adds another layer. Data and evidence-based decision making jumped from being a top priority for 17% of NFPs in 2023 to 44% in 2025. The sector is waking up to operational rigour in a way I haven't seen before. But only 23% of organisations have a documented cybersecurity plan, and only 14% have an AI governance framework, even as AI adoption has doubled. The appetite is there but the infrastructure isn't keeping pace.

The Feathr State of Nonprofit Marketing 2025 adds a layer that I think the sector is still underestimating. Email engagement rates for nonprofits remain significantly higher than social media across all age groups, including younger donors. And their data on Gen Z engagement points strongly toward YouTube and Instagram as acquisition channels, not just awareness ones. An organisation without a basic email platform and without any presence on those channels isn't just behind on tools. It's outside the conversation that's already shaping where donor relationships are forming.

The M+R Benchmarks 2026 study puts the channel picture plainly. Across 180 participating nonprofits, online revenue rose 15% in 2025. Email revenue rose 16%. Mobile revenue surged 48%. The organisations capturing that growth weren't the ones with the cleverest campaigns. They were the ones with the digital infrastructure to receive donor intent when it arrived. The data on team size is telling too: small nonprofits (under $1 million in online revenue) had an average digital team of 5, compared to 13 at extra-large organisations. The capacity gap between organisations is significant, and it shows up in the revenue.

The Australian Government's stated target of doubling philanthropic giving by 2030 isn't tracking. The Productivity Commission's Future Foundations for Giving report (Inquiry Report No. 104, May 2024) projected total giving to grow by around 48% by 2029-30 if the current nominal growth rate of 7.9% holds. That's roughly half of what doubling would require. The sector is enormous. Donations are stalling against the policy ambition. The gap between organisations capturing donor income and organisations watching it pass them by keeps widening.

The infrastructure decision

Organisations earning stable donor income aren't doing it by accident. The Fundraising Institute of Australia has documented this for years. It's deliberate donor care, segmented communications, and consistent evidence of impact. None of that happens without strategic infrastructure underneath it.

An email platform that lets you say thank you to a new donor the day they give. A list segmented well enough to send different messages to a first-time supporter and a five-year monthly giver. A website that gets updated more than once a year. A social presence that exists. These aren't optional extras. They're the operational bedrock of donor engagement.

Tip

Most email platforms offer serious nonprofit discounts. Mailchimp's nonprofit pricing gives a 15% discount and a free tier up to 500 contacts. Campaign Monitor offers 15% for verified nonprofits. The cost barrier organisations think they're facing is often a fraction of what they assume.

The part I find hardest to let go of is that when I raise the idea of a basic email platform with small nonprofits, something in the range of $50 a month, the response is almost always the same: no budget, not a priority. And yet those same organisations will pour weeks into a single grant application, complete with conditions, reporting timelines, and a funder relationship that can end next cycle. The imbalance is hard to look away from once you see it.

What I'm describing isn't a budget problem. It's a strategic decision about what counts as foundational. And in a sector where individual donations have flatlined and concentrated grant dependency is the default for small organisations, that decision has consequences.

Before adding more, audit the foundation

There's a pattern I keep noticing in small organisations. Programmes get added on top of programmes. New initiatives sit on top of older ones. The back-office systems and platforms underneath rarely get updated to match. Each new layer borrows capacity from the layer beneath it. Eventually, the foundation can't hold what's been built on it.

Before adding the next thing, the question is whether the foundation is actually solid. Three diagnostic questions worth sitting with:

  1. 01
    What percentage of your income comes from your single largest funding source?

    If one funder, one grant programme, or one philanthropic relationship withdrew tomorrow, what happens in the next ninety days?

  2. 02
    When was the last time someone outside your team gave you money, and how did they hear about you?

    If you can't answer that, you're flying blind on the donor side.

  3. 03
    If you had to send a thank-you message to every individual supporter from the past twelve months tomorrow morning, what would that actually take?

    If the answer is "we couldn't, we don't have their details," that's a critical gap.

Key takeaways

What to take from this

  • Grant funding feels secure because it's predictable. But concentrated dependency on grants is financial exposure, not stability.
  • Strip out the single $4.9 billion Minderoo donation and Australian charity sector donations rose less than 0.4% in 2023. The organisations capturing donor income growth are the ones with the digital infrastructure to receive it.
  • An email platform, a maintained website, and a segmented donor list aren't optional extras. They're the operational bedrock of a resilient funding mix. The cost is far lower than most organisations assume.

The nonprofits coming out of 2026 in a strong position aren't going to be the biggest, or the loudest, or the ones with the cleverest campaigns. They're going to be the ones that treated their operational infrastructure with the same seriousness they treat their mission.
